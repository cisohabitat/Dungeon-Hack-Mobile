Karrathal

Hall of Heroes

Score rewards depth above all: twice your experience, plus 100 for every floor reached. Taking the Heart adds half as much again, and 2000 more. Gold hoarded counts for nothing.

Relic Codex

Every named relic your heroes have picked up or bought, kept from one delve to the next.

Bestiary

What your heroes have learned about the things below, kept from one delve to the next. Meet a monster to see it; kill one to take its measure; see its trick, then beat it, to learn the answer.

How to Play

You are alone in a dungeon generated from a seed. Go down floor by floor, find the Heart of the Mountain on the deepest floor, and take it from the thing that guards it.

The same seed with the same choices makes the same dungeon. When a run ends, Share this run passes on a line with its seed and any choices you changed (through your phone's share sheet, or copied if it has none), so a friend can try the same halls. Share a picture makes a card of the run instead: the hero, how far they got and what brought them down (or the Heart they lifted), to send or to keep.

Who you are

Pick a class and a background. The class decides what you can wield and cast. The background is the life you had before the first stair: it gives you one lasting advantage, colours the opening, and decides how your story is told at the end.

Moving

The d-pad turns you and steps you one square at a time. Walk into doors to open them, into locked doors to use a matching key, and into a staircase to take it. Stairs are set into the walls, so you have to be facing them: if you step up beside a staircase the log says which side it is on, and the Use button changes its name to say what it will do — Descend, Open, Trade and so on. Hold a button to keep walking. You can also swipe on the view: left or right to turn, up or down to step. Tap the view to act on what is in front of you. Left-handed? Menu › Controls swaps the d-pad and the buttons to the other sides.

Finds

Walking onto a square picks up what lies there, except what you put down yourself and gear your class can never use: those stay where they lie and show in the Take row at the foot of the view whenever you stand on them. A tap on the row lifts everything, and a trader will buy all you cannot use in one go.

A barrel, crate or urn breaks to a blow when there is nothing to fight in front of you, or to Use (the button says Break), and walking into one kicks it over. Most hold nothing; now and then a little gold, a meal, or very rarely a healing draught.

If the view's movement bothers you, Calm view under Menu stops the shake when a blow lands, the sway and bob of your hands, the drifting dust, the shimmer of standing water and the flicker of the torches. It starts on if your phone asks for less motion.

The music follows the fight: quiet bells while nothing is near, a low pulse when something awake is close, a beat once it comes to blows, and a horn for a champion, a shade or the last foe (in the Warlord's hall, his war-drum leads it). When the fight is over it settles and goes quiet for a while, and it draws back whenever the game is paused. Music in the Menu turns it off, and Sound turns off everything.

Secrets

Some walls hide secret doors leading to treasure vaults. Walk into a suspicious wall or press Use facing it to search. Thieves often notice them just by walking past. Fountains set into the walls restore you fully, once.

Survival

Walking makes you hungry. Eat rations from your pack or you will start to starve. Your wounds close on their own only up to half your life (more for the Heartsworn); past that it takes a potion, a prayer or a Rest. Resting costs food and is impossible with enemies close by. The first rest on a floor restores everything; the second does half as much, the third a quarter, and after that the dark is too close to sleep until you find the next floor. The longer you linger, the likelier something finds you asleep. The Rest button shows how much the next rest will do; with anything close it dims and says foes near, and it never drinks for you. Drink in a fight from the Pack, or in one tap: anyone carrying a healing potion they know gets a red bottle button beside the life bar. Watch for traps in corridors. Now and then someone who knows a floor tells you where its traps lie: they show on the map as a red ×, and you step round them.

The deep takes the measure of whoever comes down. Arrive on a floor far stronger than most who reach it and what waits there is readier for you: sturdier, surer, and more often a champion. Clearing every corner still pays, just not for free.

Keyboard

W/S or arrows to move, A/D to turn, Q/E to sidestep, Space to attack, U to use, C to cast, R to rest, X to drink a healing potion, Z to read a scroll (or coat a bare blade as a fight starts), M map, I pack, P spells, H hero, J journal, Esc to close.

On a phone, the back gesture closes whatever is open, and in the dungeon it opens the Menu (pausing the game) rather than leaving it. Put the phone away with something awake close by and you come back to the Menu too, with the fight waiting for you.

Fighting

Everything happens in real time. Attack strikes the square in front of you. Monsters that reach you will hit back; step away, close doors behind you, or fight in corridors where only one can reach you. A red chevron at the edge of the view marks anything close on your flanks or behind you: it pulses when the thing is right beside you. A blow from one you cannot see lights that side of the view red for a moment, and on a phone that can, buzzes twice. A creature that wakes up growls a moment before it attacks, so turn toward the sound. Thieves move quietly, so monsters notice them late, and they deal double damage to sleeping foes.

Warning marks

No blow comes without warning. A monster about to strike draws back and a warning mark appears over it, a triangle that fills from the bottom and turns from yellow to red as the blow comes; you also hear it. Step back or sidestep before it lands and it hits empty air — mostly. A few follow a step straight back: a rat pounces, a ghoul or a wraith lunges after you, and the lich's touch reaches two squares down a line. Against those, step to the side. Goblins and orcs never draw back the same way twice, so watch the mark, not the rhythm. An archer drawing its bow misses if you step out of its line. A blow coming from your side or behind makes the red chevron at that edge flash fast.

The dice

Every swing is a twenty-sided die plus your bonus against the target's armour class, and the log can show the working: (d20 14+5 vs AC 13). A natural one always misses and a natural twenty is always a critical hit (the log calls it a telling blow), so those say so instead of printing a sum. Monsters roll the same way against your armour class, and their blows show it too, except that on the first two floors a monster's natural twenty still hits but does not do double damage. Watch the numbers to learn what you are up against — if your bonus is barely clearing its armour, something heavier or a different approach will serve you better, and if everything is landing on you, your armour has fallen behind the depth. The working is hidden to begin with: turn on Combat rolls under Menu to see it.

Two blades

A Fighter can put a second light weapon — a dagger, club or short sword — in the off hand instead of a shield. Open the pack, choose the blade and press Off hand. The second blade follows every blow of the first, hit or miss, but it swings wilder (wilder still when the first has missed and left you off balance) and carries none of your strength, and holding two slows your main hand. The second blade parries too, for one point of armour class, less than any shield but a buckler. It clears a room of soft targets faster than anything else; against something well armoured a two-handed weapon still lands the better blow, and a real shield still keeps you alive longer.

A Thief carries no shield but a buckler, the smallest, strapped to the forearm where it does not slow the hands: one more point of armour class. Anything bigger is too much for the work.

The Ranger

A Ranger fights with a bow: an arrow flies to the first foe down the corridor ahead, and Dexterity lands it and adds to it, whatever the weapon. A shot from a bow or a sling at a foe two squares off or more bites harder (Steady Aim, +1; not throwing knives, which are thrown, not aimed), so the ranger's art is keeping the distance, and a bow's long reach gives the most shots before anything arrives. The pack counts Steady Aim when it compares a bow with a blade, and says how much further the bow reaches, which its damage figure cannot count.

Light armour, no shield (a bow needs both hands), and a dagger, a sword or a spear for when something gets close; like a thief, a ranger grows harder to hit with every few levels.

On the Cast button, Snare throws a weighted cord at the first foe down the corridor, five squares at most: it stands caught for two and a half seconds, and any blow it was drawing back is broken off. It comes back after sixteen seconds.

A Sharpshooter's arrows bite harder still from three squares off, their critical hits come a number sooner, their first arrow at a foe that has not seen them never misses, and their snare reaches two squares further; a Warden is harder to hit, their snare bites, holds a second longer and comes back three seconds sooner, and a snared foe takes 2 more from every blow and arrow of theirs.

The Druid

A Druid keeps the old ways, and Wisdom answers them: it lands the druid's blows (with a spear, a staff, a club or a dagger), as faith does a cleric's, and fills their spell points. Light armour and a buckler at most.

Wild Shape is a spell like the others, and makes a bear of you for forty seconds: you strike with its claws (1d8, and Wisdom or Strength behind them) instead of what you hold, your armour class is 2 better, and its hide takes the blows before you do, a few points thicker every level (the status line shows the time and the hide left). It ends when its time runs out, when a blow tears through the hide (the rest of that blow reaches you), or when you cast any other spell: a bear cannot say the words, so the druid lets it go to speak. What rides on your weapon, a relic's power or an oil, stays behind with it while you are a bear, and so does your shield: a bear holds nothing, so a rustmaw finds no blade or shield on it to eat. The hide keeps out blows, darts and falling stone, not hunger or poison, which work from inside. A bear can still drink a draught or read a scroll.

The other spells: Thorn Lash, a whip of thorns at the first foe within four squares; Mending Moss, which heals you and your companion alike; Entangle, roots that hold the first foe ahead for three seconds and break off the blow it was drawing back (a boss tears free in half the time); Call Lightning; and at seventh level Insect Plague, a swarm that fills the corridor ahead.

A companion at a druid's side is half again as tough, comes to them a floor further on in what it knows, and is mended by their healing. A Shapeshifter takes the bear a point more cheaply and for longer, with harder claws, but finds the words for every other spell a point dearer; a Grovewarden is harder to hit, their companion is twice as tough as at another's side and bites harder, their moss heals more, their thorns hold back what they strike and their roots hold longer. The Druid comes to your fire after a win on one life with a companion still at your side. Two relics are made for druids alone: Oakheart, a staff whose Wild power lengthens the bear and thickens its hide (it goes into the shape as you take it, though the bear holds nothing), and the Mossmantle, living moss over soft hide that mends you and keeps poison out. A druid knows the old stone, the pale caps and the lie of the land, and has a better chance with each.

Class moves

A Fighter and a Thief have no spells, but each has a move of their own on the Cast button, which comes back after a few seconds. The fighter's Bash strikes with shield or pommel: it breaks off any blow or trick the foe in front is drawing back and sets it reeling back a moment (a shield rings it harder; a Knight's sets it back longer; a Berserker's bash is a blow of its own). It comes back after fifteen seconds. The thief's Smoke makes everything within three squares lose them for three seconds, as good as asleep: slip away, or land the double blow a sleeping foe takes. A blow already being drawn back still comes, so throw it before the mark, not after (an Assassin's smoke hangs longer; a Trickster's comes back sooner). The lich sees through smoke. Each has a talent to build on it: a fighter's Shield Slam brings Bash back in ten seconds and knocks the foe a square back, dazed a second longer, and a thief's Choking Cloud leaves whatever lost them in the smoke coughing, its first move a second and a half late.

Magic

Mages and Clerics have spell points that return slowly as you walk, or fully when you Rest. Cast repeats your last spell. Casting takes time, like a swing: a mage's words are quick, a cleric's prayers slower, and you cannot swing and cast at the same moment. Anyone can read scrolls from their pack.

The Heart

The Heart of the Mountain waits on the deepest floor, held fast by the Dread Lich: it will not come loose while the lich stands. It is the delve's last and hardest fight: on Normal and Hard it hits surer and harder than the creatures of its floor would, and on any difficulty fewer of them stand between you and it. The lich fights in three acts. At two thirds of its life it raises guards and throws grave-cold from behind them; at one third it calls a wraith, puts out its torches and tries to drink the Heart's light to mend itself — strike it to break the rite. A vigil lamp burns in the room nearest its hall, for the gold you have left. Bring it down, lift the Heart, and the run is won.

Take the Warrens at the divided stair and it is not the lich at the bottom but the Goblin Warlord, who has taken the Heart's hall and keeps it chained to his hoard. He hits as hard as the lich, on the same terms, and fights in three acts of his own. He beats a war-drum to call a pair of goblins, twice at most: strike him while the stick is raised and the beat dies. At two thirds he leaps onto his throne behind two orc shield-bearers, who turn every blow meant for him, and throws spears from it: cut them down and he must come down. A spell flies over their heads and reaches him there, and his spears break on a mage's Shield. At one third he kicks open his war-chest and fights in a frenzy, quicker than before. When he falls his warband runs, and his hoard is yours.

Groups

From the second floor down, goblins, rats, skeletons and bats go about in groups of two, and deeper in threes, sharing one square. You fight the one in front; when it falls the next steps up, and the square is clear only when the last is down. They draw back together and swing in a quick volley, every one of them, so a group hits hard; step away while the warning mark is up and the whole volley misses. And a spell that fills the square hits every one of them at once: a mage's Burning Hands, Lightning Bolt and Cone of Cold, a cleric's Flame Strike, and a Scroll of Fire. Lightning and cold carry on down the corridor into any group behind.

Monster tricks

Many monsters have a trick of their own. It gets a bigger warning mark, a violet spiked burst rather than a triangle, a deeper sound and a line in the log, and every trick has an answer — usually a step, sometimes a weapon or a spell. The first time you meet each trick, and the first time anything swings at you, a tip says what to do and time runs slow until the moment has passed (unless tips are off). The very first tip, about the controls, holds the dungeon still while you read it, until you move or it fades. A few monsters have a nature rather than a trick, like the troll that grows its wounds back. Your Bestiary, in the Journal and on the title screen, writes each trick down once you have seen it, and the answer once you have beaten it. Tap a creature's picture there to look closer.

The way down

Choose 12 or 16 floors when you make your hero for the Long Delve: the same dungeon drawn out, so every kind of creature has floors of its own before the lich's (the trolls, the ogres and the minotaur come in their turn rather than all at the bottom), and three named champions hold floors on the way down instead of two. It is the harder road: from the seventh floor its creatures hit surer, and every floor past the sixth they are a little sturdier. On Hard the deep answers magic too: from the seventh floor spells strike and heal 6% harder for every floor past the sixth, and so do a cleric's blows. A win on it, on Normal or Hard, is a feat with its own trophy in the Hall.

About a third of the way down, the stair divides. Take it and you choose: down into the Crypts, old burial halls where the dead and the spiders keep house, or into the Warrens, goblin tunnels and orc halls with bigger things further down. The floors that follow, until the last two, lean that way: their walls (the dead stacked skull on skull in the Crypts, dug earth propped with timber in the Warrens), their creatures, the champions that hold them, what the traders keep, one encounter met nowhere else, and on the road's last floor a relic found nowhere else: the Sexton's Locket down the Crypts, the Warchief's Knuckle down the Warrens. The roads meet again near the bottom. A win by each road is a feat of its own.

Now and then a middle floor is not like the others, and the log says so as you arrive: a dark floor has most of its torches out (harder to see, but sleeping things notice you a square later), a flooded one has everything wading, you too, a quarter slower, the restless dead have raised many of a floor's creatures as undead, a goblin market means a trader who sells cheaper than most, and an overgrown floor, roots through the stone and moss over everything, hides its traps under the moss (harder to spot, except for a druid, who reads it), grows pale caps you can eat, and is a druid's own ground: their spells cost a point less there. A chip at the top of the view reminds you while you are there.

Named champions

About a third of the way down, and again about two thirds, one floor is held by a named champion: a goblin king, a web-mother, an orc warchief, a ghoul lord and worse, each one of its kind grown great. The log names it when you arrive, and it sleeps in a room of its own, away from the stairs, with some of its kin. It is bigger than its kind and washed in its own colour, and once it wakes its life runs across the top of the view. Each has its kind's trick sharpened — webs twice as often, a claw every other blow, a horn to call its kin — warned of and answered like any other. Bring it down and it leaves something fine where it falls: a relic the traders were keeping for you, or a well-made piece and a purse of gold.

Your dead come back. The last hero you lost outside the Daily Delve is remembered on this device. In a later run (again, not a daily one) their bones lie on the floor where they fell, still wearing their weapon and armour and, if they wore one, a ring or an amulet, and their shade keeps watch over them. The log warns you when you arrive. The shade is as strong as its floor and fights as its hero did: a fighter's charges, a mage's throws cold fire, a cleric's mends itself, a thief's is quick and follows a step back, a ranger's shoots. Lay it to rest and the gear is yours, though you will not know how good it is until you study it, and a curse it carried is carried still. A relic does not come back with them: each is one of a kind. They are never met on the first floor or the lich's: a death on the first floor waits on the second, and one on the lich's floor or deeper waits on the floor above it. A newer death takes the older one's place. The Hall of Heroes says who is still down there.

Fire, cold and lightning

Burning Hands, Flame Strike, a Scroll of Fire, a flaming blade and a mage's Kindling are fire; Cone of Cold is cold; Lightning Bolt is lightning. Some things take half as much again from one of them and some only half: rot and web burn well, the dead barely feel the cold, and metal in the belly draws the lightning. The first time you find out, the number over the creature says weak! or resists and the log says why, and the Bestiary remembers. The cold works both ways: a wraith's touch, the lich's grave-cold and its storm are cold, and a Ring of Warmth halves them.

Your six scores

Your six scores are rolled when you make your hero, the best of them in your class's key score; roll again as often as you like. Or choose Point buy: every score starts at 8, and 27 points raise them to 17 at most, dearer near the top. Some lessons raise a score as you level, and Self-Taught gives two points to put wherever you choose, though no score takes more than two from it in a run.

Every score matters, fighting or not. Strength lands harder blows, forces doors, keeps your feet when something charges into you and tears you out of a grip. Dexterity keeps you out of reach, your fingers quick, dodges a trap that goes off under you (a pit is only half a fall), and slips you out of a grip if you are quicker than you are strong; it also halves a web or a storm of fire that catches you. Constitution is your hit points and your stomach, and fights off venom, a numbing claw and a gaze of stone. These are saves: a d20 and the score, harder the deeper you go. A monster's trick is still best answered by getting out of its way; a save only softens what lands. Intelligence powers a mage's spells and lets anyone work out an unknown potion or scroll — choose Study in your pack rather than finding out by drinking it. Wisdom powers a cleric's prayers, spots traps before you step on them and holds your life together against the drain of the dead. Charisma changes what traders charge you and pay you, and talks you through many encounters. Your class and background give a knack for some things: a thief picks locks well, a cleric's prayers are heard, the tombwise read old writing.

Growing

Experience raises your hit points and unlocks spells on its own. Every level also teaches you something: you choose one of three small lessons, a point of strength, a few hit points, a surer eye. On every even level you choose a talent instead, one of three from your class's own. Talents change how you fight — a fighter's Cleave that carries into the next of a group, a mage's Mirror Image, a thief's Shadow Step that turns a sidestep into a blow from the shadows — and each is yours for the rest of the run. Your talents and lessons are listed under Hero.

At level 5 every class chooses its path, in place of that level's lesson: a fighter becomes a Knight, shield up and hard to move, or a Berserker who hits harder the more they bleed and guards less; a cleric a Templar who smites harder, blesses for longer and bites the undead, or a Healer; a mage a Pyromancer whose fire burns on, or a Frostweaver whose cold holds things back; a thief an Assassin who strikes from the dark, or a Trickster who is never quite where the blow lands; a ranger a Sharpshooter, deadliest from afar, or a Warden whose snare holds the line; a druid a Shapeshifter, more bear than not, or a Grovewarden, who keeps their companion and the green things. Each path changes two or three things, listed on the level-up card and under Hero, and it is chosen once, for the rest of the run. At level 9 a hero on a path masters it, again in place of the lesson: one of two capstones that make the path's own gift stronger, such as a Knight's shield that catches one blow in four, or a Healer's miracle that once on each floor turns a killing blow into half their life back.

Encounters

Not everything down here wants to fight. On most floors something waits to be dealt with: a survivor under fallen stone, a shrine that still takes offerings, words cut into a wall, a voice in the dark that knows your name. Walk up to it or press Examine. Each choice says what it tests and how likely it is to go well — Strength: 55% chance, and with Combat rolls on, the dice behind it too — and some cost gold or blood before you roll. There is always a way to walk away, and you have to choose one: once you have looked, the moment has to be answered. Each encounter is met at most once in a run.

On the second floor you may find a starving hound. Share your food with it (a meal from your pack, or, with none, a quarter of your hunger bar and never the last of it), or win it over, and it follows you for the rest of the run: it bites whatever stands beside it, takes the blows of anything that reaches it before you, grows as you do, and heals when you rest (a hurt hound is reason enough to rest). If you walk on, it leaves the fight and comes after you. Face it and press Use to tell it to stay (it will wait there, even if you go down the stair) or to come; walk into it and it steps into your place. A hound at your heel is not quiet: sleeping things hear you a square sooner, so tell it to stay when you mean to creep up on something. If it falls, it is gone for the run. When it is hurt or waiting, its name shows under your bars, and your map marks where it is.

In a delve with no hound, you may find a caged goblin about halfway down instead. Let it out (wrench the bars, or pick the padlock) and it follows you as the hound does, though it is no fighter: it stabs, it takes blows badly, and it is quiet on its feet. Its gifts are its fingers. Face a locked door with no key while it is near and press Pick: it opens the lock, quietly. And it makes safe any trap it comes within two squares of, and lends its fingers to any Dexterity test at an encounter while it is close by (+2). You only ever have one companion.

A Druid in a delve with no hound is found at the first stair by a grey wolf, which follows as a hound does but hunts quietly (no panting to wake the sleepers) and learns different things: blooded, Pack Hunter; a veteran, Savage, a bite 2 harder at anything below half its life. One companion at a time: a goblin let out of its cage while another already follows you thanks you and slips off into the dark.

A companion grows with every new floor it comes down at your side (one told to stay has not come). After two it is blooded, after four a veteran: each time it is mended whole, grows a little tougher and learns a trick. A hound learns to hamstring (one bite in three that lands holds its foe back a moment), then to hunt as a pack (you have +2 to hit anything it stands beside). A goblin learns to backstab (double damage to anything at your side), then to scrounge (on each new floor it finds you some gold, a flask or a scroll). The Hero sheet says how far it has to go.

Traders sometimes keep a charm for a companion: an Iron-Studded Collar (blows find it harder to land), a Fang Charm (2 more on every blow) or a Rowan Knot (its wounds close by themselves). Choose Give to from the charm in your pack while your companion is on the floor with you. It wears one at a time, and the one it wore comes back to your pack; if it falls, what it wore is left where it fell.

Some choices follow you down. A guildsman you dig out goes ahead and marks the next floor; a captive you free puts in a word with the traders below; a crew you stop to bury is with you at the end; the Lampfolk hear of what you do for one of their own in the dark, and of what you take from them; and whatever you take from the pale thing in the narrow passage, the lich will be the stronger for. The Hero sheet lists what is still following you, and the story at the end remembers.

Gold

Traders set up shop on some floors. Most are Lampfolk: small grey people with eyes like lanterns, who have always lived in the deep and trade with anything that pays, so nothing down there harms them. On a goblin market floor the trader is a goblin pedlar, and cheaper. Walk into them to open the shop. They sell potions, scrolls, food and the occasional weapon, and buy anything you do not want, for about a quarter of what they would ask for it. Their forge will hone your weapon or reinforce your armour, up to +3, for more each time. From the fourth floor down each trader will also sell you one draught of a bitter tonic, four more hit points for good, and a caster can pay to read their books for three more spell points. For gold you can also sleep safe by the trader's lamp, once a floor: a whole night, and nothing finds you. You can carry five of any one potion. Gold you never spend counts for nothing, not even on your gravestone.

A trader may also have a job for the floor below, at the top of its services: slay the champion that keeps a lair there, kill a number of its creatures, or find a satchel one of the Lampfolk lost there (it lies far from the stair). Taking it costs nothing. It is for that floor only: go on down, or back up, with it undone and it is lost. Once it is done, the next trader you meet, on any floor below, pays in gold and a flask or a scroll. The line under the bars shows how it goes while you are on its floor, and the Hero sheet keeps it.

Relics

A few pieces of gear down here have names: Grimtooth, Rustwarden Mail, the Ogre's Toll. Each is a weapon, armour or shield you could use anyway, better made and with a power of its own — striking the undead harder, drinking back health, keeping poison out of your blood. Their names show in gold, and they hover where they lie. A handful wait on the floors of every run, always ones your class can use, usually on the pile furthest from the stairs. The traders keep the rest behind the counter for anyone with the coin. Look at one in your pack to read its powers and its story.

Three pairs of relics were made to go together, and do more worn at once: the Stairwarden's Arms (Kest's Bulwark and Rustwarden Mail, +2 armour class), the Nightwalk (Whisper and Shadowskin, a strike from the shadows hits a step harder: double damage becomes triple) and the Order of the Dawn (Dawnbringer and the Sisters' Buckler, stronger healing and more against the undead). A relic's card names its other half.

From the third floor down a well-made piece may carry a quality of its make, named in front once you know its make: a Heavy weapon deals one more damage, a True one hits one more often, Sturdy armour or a sturdy shield adds a point of armour class, and a Blessed one adds one to every saving throw. With a power as well, it reads like Heavy Mace of Flame +2. From the second floor down a trader's forge will also work a quality into the weapon or armour you wear, if it is plain and known and not a relic, for a good deal of gold: the quality on offer depends on the trader.

Every relic any of your heroes picks up or buys goes in the codex, kept from one delve to the next: open it from the Hall of Heroes or the Journal's Relics tab. The ones still missing show only whether they are a weapon, armour, a shield, a ring or an amulet, and which road a road's own relic waits down.

Ordinary gear can carry a power too. From the second floor down, some well-made pieces are of Keenness, of Thirst, of Flame, of Warding and the like, each with one power a relic might have. Like any found gear its quality is hidden until you wear it, study it or have a trader look, and a powered piece is worth far more to sell. Everything your gear gives is listed under Hero.

Oils come in squat flasks stoppered with a rag, found among the scrolls and sold by traders. Choose Coat weapon in your pack (or, as a fight starts with your blade bare, tap the flask at the left end of the log) and the oil rides on your next 20 blows that land, bow and sling included; the count is shown under the bars. Fire Oil burns for 1d4 more on every blow and stops a troll's wounds closing; Silver Wash deals 1d6 more to the undead and nothing to anything else; Blade Venom poisons the living one blow in three. A new oil wipes off the old one, and the oil stays on the weapon it was put on: take up another and it does not have it. A Crypts trader keeps more silver, a Warrens one more fire and venom.

Robes

A mage cannot wear armour, but wears robes: an Apprentice's Robe to begin with, and better ones now and then on a pile or a trader's shelf, in a mage's dungeon only. A Silk Robe adds four spell points, a Warded Robe more armour, and the rare Robe of the Magi both, with six spell points, and makes the great workings, spells of five points or more, cost one less. A found robe hides its make, as any found armour does, and can be cursed.

The free hand

A mage who puts the staff away for a dagger has a hand free for a focus: a Spellbook brings spell points back a quarter faster as you walk, a Crystal Orb adds one to each of a spell's dice, up to two, and the rare Orb of Storms drives Lightning Bolt and Cone of Cold a fifth harder and holds what they hit a moment longer. A cleric may carry a holy symbol in place of the shield, trading most of its armour (a symbol still turns one point's worth) for the faith's: a Holy Symbol makes healing spells heal a quarter more, a Silver Sunburst makes Holy Smite and Flame Strike strike a quarter harder, and a Reliquary does both. They turn up only in that caster's dungeon, and a trader may keep one a little finer than the floors around it offer, and a found one can be cursed.

Cloaks

Anyone can wear a cloak over everything else, and it is known at a glance. A Cloak of Protection gives a point of armour class on top of anything else, a Ring of Protection included; an Elven Cloak lets you pass a square closer before sleeping things notice you; a Cloak of Warmth halves what cold does to you. One turns up now and then on a pile or a trader's shelf. An Elven Cloak and a Ring of Stealth do not add up, and nor do a Cloak of Warmth and a Ring of Warmth.

The deep's own beasts leave something of themselves now and then. A fallen cave wyrm may leave its scales, laced into Wyrm-Scale Mail: light armour that halves a wyrm's fire. A fallen quillback may leave its quills on a Quill Shield, which pricks whatever strikes you in close combat. Neither is ever found lying about or sold.

Rings and amulets

Anyone can wear two rings and an amulet, whatever their class: a mage in robes can still wear a Ring of Protection. From the second floor down one turns up now and then on a pile, and traders sometimes keep one. A found one is known only by its look — a jade ring, an amber amulet — until you put it on or Study it, and which look is which changes every run. Rings of Protection, Might, Evasion (every saving throw) and the Seer (traps and hidden doors) come in amounts and can be finely made, or cursed; Regeneration, Stealth and Warmth rings, and the amulets of Warding and Wizardry, simply do what they say. Two rings of one kind do not add up: only the better one counts. An Amulet of Life Saving takes the blow that would have killed you, leaves you at half your life, and crumbles. An unknown ring sells for a trinket's price: the trader will not tell you what you had.

Curses

Gear you find keeps its quality to itself: the pack marks it with a ?. It might be finely made, it might be ordinary, and from the second floor down it might be cursed. Put it on and you find out at once, but a cursed piece will not come off again until the curse is broken. Safer ways to know: Study it from your pack (an Intelligence check), or pay a trader to appraise it. To break a curse, read a Scroll of Remove Curse, pray at a shrine, or pay a trader to lift it. Gear bought from a trader is always what it says.

The crews who went first

Four guild crews came down before you and none came back. Each floor holds one page of what they left behind, up to eight in all, so a short delve holds fewer. Pick them up and read them in the Journal. Whether you carry them out is part of how your run is remembered.

One life

Permadeath is on unless you untick it when you make your hero. The run is still saved whenever you change floors or put the game away, so you can close it and pick up where you were with Continue. What you cannot do is go back: Load Game stays shut, and when you die the save is erased. Untick permadeath for a gentler run where you can save from the menu and load it again after a death.

To carry a hero to another phone or browser, open Menu › Save code and copy the code (or save it as a file), then choose Load from code on the other one's title screen. It replaces any hero waiting there. A code cannot take back a permadeath run: this device will not load one for a hero who has already died here, or one older than how far you have played them here.

Difficulty

Choose it when you make your hero. Easy leaves more to find, and the monsters never grow with you. Normal is the delve as it was meant. Hard has sturdier monsters, thinner rests, and a last foe (the lich, or the Warlord) at full strength; from the fourth floor down its creatures hit surer still.

Trophies

Claim the Heart and your class earns a trophy for that difficulty: six classes, three difficulties, eighteen in all, shown at the top of the Hall of Heroes. A win with each of the twelve paths is a trophy too, and so are the feats: the Long Delve, a win by each road (by the Warrens, past the Goblin Warlord), finding every relic over all your runs (the Collector), a win with three traders' jobs done in the run (Friend of the Lampfolk), a win with a veteran companion still at your side (Old Campaigners), and a druid's win having taken the bear's shape thirty times in the run (Wildheart). A class won on Hard earns its title, worn on its card from then on: Blademaster, High Priest, Archmage, Shadowmaster, Deepstalker, Archdruid. The Ranger comes to your fire once the other four classes have each won on one life, at any difficulty; its card on the New Game screen says which are still to win. The Druid comes after a win with a companion still at your side. Daily runs count as well. Two backgrounds are earned this way. The Returned opens after any win on Normal or Hard, and The Heartsworn after a win on Hard; until then they show on the New Game screen as locked, with how to earn them.

After any win on Hard, vows open on the New Game screen: the Iron Vow (no resting, not even by a trader's lamp), the Pauper's Vow (no trader will deal with you) and the Unaided Vow (no draught passes your lips). Swear as many as you like; each one kept to a win on one life, on Normal or Hard, is a trophy of its own. The Daily Delve takes no vows.

The Daily Delve

Everyone gets the same dungeon each day, with the same hero: class, background and scores all come from the date. Eight floors, normal difficulty, one life, and one try. When it is over the title screen shows how you did, and Share passes on a one-line result, through your phone's share sheet or copied to paste wherever you like. Play on consecutive days to build a streak.

The Stair Divides
